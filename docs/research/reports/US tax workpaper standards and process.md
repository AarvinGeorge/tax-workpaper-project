# US rules make every 1040 number prove itself

No US standard says what a tax workpaper must contain. Audits have one: PCAOB AS 1215 and AICPA AU-C 230 set out what an audit file must hold. For an individual (Form 1040) return, the file's contents are instead set indirectly, by about five overlapping rule sets. The first is the AICPA's revised Statements on Standards for Tax Services (SSTS, effective 1 January 2024). The second is Treasury Circular 230. The third is a handful of Internal Revenue Code penalty and record rules (§6694, §6695 with Form 8867, §6107, §7216). The fourth is the IRS e-file rulebook, Publication 1345. The fifth is the FTC Safeguards Rule. Their shared demand is the one the tool's design already assumes. Every number on the return must be traceable to where it came from. Any inconsistency must end in a documented question and answer. Review must be recorded as who checked what and when. The signed return must be locked so it cannot change. Traditional CPA practice meets these demands with a paper-shaped **binder**: an index in 1040 order, **lead sheets** (one summary page per area), **tick marks** (small symbols meaning, for example, "agreed to source document") and initialled **sign-offs**. Its main weakness is that a sign-off does not reopen when a corrected 1099 or a late K-1 changes a number. That gap, together with the precise "tamper-proof after signing" and "$50 / $14 re-signature" rules in Pub 1345, gives the event-log design a clear advantage. Most of the core design can be shown in a one-day prototype. The security and e-file controls (MFA, encryption, consent forms, transmission) can be described rather than built.

## A 1040 file is a binder built so a stranger can trace any number

A CPA firm's file for one household's return has three layers. The **binder** is the whole engagement. A **lead sheet** summarises one area, such as wages, interest and dividends, or Schedule D, and ties each return line to its support. The **workpaper** is the detail behind a number: the source document, the calculation, the notes ([Soraban](https://www.soraban.com/accounting-workpapers)). The binder is organised by an index that "follows the flow of a 1040 tax return". Scanning products such as Thomson Reuters SurePrep 1040SCAN and SPbinder build that index by bookmarking each scanned document into it ([Thomson Reuters SPbinder](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/en/products/spbinder.html); [SurePrep 1040SCAN](https://Tax.ThomsonReuters.com/en/products/sureprep-1040scan)). In the paper era, "preparers used workpaper leadsheets to tie amounts on 1040 tax returns to their related source documents" ([Thomson Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/en/insights/white-papers/maximize-review-efficiency-with-spbinders-work-paper-lead-sheets/form)). That tie-in was done with **tick marks** and **cross-references**, a hand-drawn link from an amount on one page to its support on another. The IRS's own examiner manual sets the discipline for both. Tick marks need not be standard, but "they must be consistent throughout the workpapers for an issue" and must be explained in a legend. Every lead-sheet line carries a "Workpaper Reference" to the support that explains the work done ([IRM 4.10.9.8.2](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-010-009)). A **prior-year comparison** sits beside the binder. SPbinder's two-year sheet shows current year, prior year, and the change in dollars and percent ([Thomson Reuters help](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/sureprep/spbinder/leadsheets/prior-year-amounts-in-leadsheets)).

A few more terms recur through this report. **Engagement letter** is the signed contract stating which returns the firm will prepare. **Organizer** and **PBC list** ("prepared by client") are the questionnaire and the checklist of documents the client owes. A **diagnostic** is the tax software's own error or warning message. An **ERO** (electronic return originator) is the firm authorised to e-file. **Form 8879** is the taxpayer's signed authorisation to e-file. **MeF** is the IRS's Modernised e-File system. **Carryforward** means an amount that persists across years, such as a capital loss, a passive loss or an asset's basis.

The season runs as a chain of handoffs. It starts with the engagement letter and §7216 consent, then the organizer and PBC list. Next comes document intake, by portal or by scanning with OCR into a bookmarked binder. Data entry or import into the tax software follows, then diagnostics and layered review, with client questions in between. If the return is not ready by the deadline, the firm files an extension. Then come client approval and the Form 8879 signature, e-filing with IRS acknowledgment or reject handling, delivery of the client copy, and archiving. The Tax Adviser's risk guidance asks for engagement letters that name every return, including each state return by name. They should be re-signed every year and reissued when a service is added, and e-file acknowledgments should be tracked daily ([The Tax Adviser, Aug 2021](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2021/aug/risk-mitigation-best-practices/)). Almost every step runs in a separate product: portal, scanner, tax engine, binder, e-signature, e-file. The context behind a number, meaning who said what and why, leaks out at every handoff.

The file is written for readers who come later. They include next year's preparer, internal reviewers, IRS examiners, and the firm's lawyers. That last reader matters most commercially. About **66% of claims** in the AICPA professional liability programme in 2020 related to tax services, most often improper treatment or advice, or late filing ([Journal of Accountancy, Feb 2021](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2021/feb/cpa-firm-liability-advice-defending-tax-and-other-claims.html)). Firm tax-practice reviews judge work on three tests: whether it "was substantively correct, was documented in the files and was communicated to the client in understandable form" ([Journal of Accountancy, Dec 2000](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2000/dec/qualitycontrolfortaxpractices/)). A "self-contained" file is one where a stranger can rebuild every number and every judgement without the preparer in the room.

## Review runs in layers, and a sign-off never reopens when the number changes

Review runs in layers. The preparer finishes the return. A senior or manager does a detail review and sends **review notes** back. The preparer answers them. The reviewer clears each note, signs off on the workpapers used, and passes the file to the signing partner ([CPA Trendlines](https://cpatrendlines.com/shop/htrtr/)). Software builds these steps in. SPbinder supports "up to four sign-off levels" ([Thomson Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/en/products/spbinder.html)). CCH Axcess Tax lets a reviewer attach a note to a field, a worksheet or the whole return, and sorts diagnostics into four classes: e-file errors that must be cleared, warnings, cautions and informational alerts. It also lists every **override** (a number typed over the computed one) and every **estimate** so each can be cleared ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/nl-nl/expert-insights/onscreen-review-is-the-next-critical-stage-for-tax-workflow-improvement)). Lacerte goes further. A fatal diagnostic blocks printing and e-filing, and a reviewer's "reviewed" tick does not resolve the diagnostic underneath ([Intuit ProConnect community](https://proconnect.intuit.com/community/individual/help/lacerte-informational-diagnostics-frequently-asked-questions/00/5356)). Reviewers work from exceptions first: diagnostics, overrides and estimates, then large swings against the prior year. The NATP advises running a two-year comparison and investigating anything without a logical explanation ([NATP](https://www.natptax.com/news-insights/blog/solo-tax-preparers-need-a-sharper-second-look-before-filing/)).

A useful review note records the issue, the assumption made, what was changed and how it was resolved. "A note marked 'open' should mean the team still needs action" ([Soraban](https://www.soraban.com/accounting-workpapers)). So a review note is really a small workflow object: it has a scope, an owner, a state and a resolution. Paper and PDF hold it as loose text.

Practitioners' complaints point to the gap. Returns stall because "the bottleneck isn't capacity; it's clarity" about who reviews what ([Intuit ProConnect](https://accountants.intuit.com/taxprocenter/practice-management/workflow-tools/bottlenecks-that-make-tax-season-harder)). Documents arrive in pieces, "one document today, … a photo tomorrow", and missing context found late forces rework ([Soraban](https://www.soraban.com/accounting-workpapers)). The structural flaw follows from how sign-offs work. A sign-off is a set of initials and a date on a page, so when a corrected 1099-B arrives after review, the return changes but the sign-off and the review notes still refer to the old value. None of the products researched reopens a sign-off at the level of a single number. Software engineering solved the same problem in code review. GitHub's protected branches can "dismiss stale pull request approvals when commits are pushed" and "require conversation resolution before merging" ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-protected-branches/about-protected-branches)). These two rules are exactly the tool's "sign-offs reopen on change" and "review notes block filing".

## No US standard prescribes tax workpapers; five rule sets shape them indirectly

The US has no authoritative tax workpaper standard. This is the central finding for a designer, and it is good news: the file's structure is a product decision, not a template the law imposes. The AICPA's firm-level quality standard, **SQMS No. 1** (effective 15 December 2025), covers the firm's "accounting and auditing practice", meaning audits, reviews, compilations and similar work ([Linford & Co.](https://linfordco.com/blog/aicpa-quality-management-standards-sqms-1/); [Oregon Society of CPAs](https://www.orcpa.org/news/6c89ad9b-b4f2-46c9-8d13-1314235fe5f8:new-standards-for-quality-management-in-your-accounting-or-auditing-practice)). AICPA peer review "does not cover tax" ([FICPA](https://ficpa.org/storage/files/c9ccc2b97159060b4dba3410f740d578.pdf)). So a tax-only practice is generally outside both. That is an inference from how they define their scope; no AICPA text says so in those words. What governs tax documentation is a set of duties that require evidence, without saying what format the evidence takes.

**The AICPA SSTS (revised, effective 1 January 2024)** are enforceable on AICPA members, and many state boards adopt them ([AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/download/revised-statements-on-standards-for-tax-services-no-1-4-1-1-2024); [SSTS FAQ](https://assets.ctfassets.net/rb9cdnjh59cm/59x5bb0KdXzhm4TLCs4zBp/3d6e7a15cf5652ba1302d99af5d1aeda/faq-for-statements-on-standards-for-tax-services-ssts-effective_1-1-2024.pdf)). They contain no documentation standard and no supervision standard. They shape the workpaper through the following sections ([SSTS text](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/AICPA_ssts_no_1_4.pdf)).

- **§2.3, reliance on information from others.** This is the heart of the workpaper. A member "may, in good faith, rely on information furnished, without verification". But the member "should not ignore the implications of information furnished" and "should make reasonable inquiries if the information furnished appears to be incorrect, incomplete, or inconsistent either on its face or on the basis of other facts known". The member should also consult prior-year returns "whenever feasible" to "avoid the omission or duplication of items".
- **§2.4, estimates.** Estimates are allowed where exact data is impractical to get, but they must not "imply greater accuracy than exists".
- **§2.2, return questions.** Every required question on the return, such as the digital-asset question, must be answered or have a stated reason for leaving it blank.
- **§1.2, knowledge of errors.** An error found in a prior-year return must be reported to the client promptly, whoever prepared that return. The member must "take reasonable steps to ensure that the error is not repeated".
- **§1.3, data protection.** Members must safeguard data and hold only what is needed.
- **§1.4, reliance on tools.** Tools include AI. A member may rely on them, but "use of a tool does not absolve the member of professional obligations", and the 1040 perjury statement "cannot be transferred entirely to reliance on a tool".

**Treasury Circular 230 (31 CFR Part 10)** binds everyone who practises before the IRS. It mirrors SSTS §2.3 almost word for word. §10.34(d) allows good-faith reliance but requires reasonable inquiries when information appears incorrect, inconsistent or incomplete ([31 CFR 10.34](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.34)). It adds the clause that gives review its legal value. Under §10.22 a practitioner relying on another person's work is "presumed to have exercised due diligence" if they used "reasonable care in engaging, supervising, training, and evaluating" that person ([31 CFR 10.22](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.22)). So a recorded review is the evidence that earns that presumption. §10.36 requires the partner in charge to keep "adequate procedures in effect" across the firm ([31 CFR 10.36](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.36)). §10.21 requires telling the client promptly about an error ([31 CFR 10.21](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.21)). §10.28 requires returning the client's own records on request, but not the firm's analysis ([31 CFR 10.28](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.28)).

**The Internal Revenue Code's preparer rules** set the penalties that make documentation worth keeping. Under **§6694**, a preparer is penalised for a position without **substantial authority** if it is undisclosed, or without a **reasonable basis** if it is disclosed. These are tiers of legal support, from strongest to weakest. The penalty is the greater of $1,000 or 50% of the fee ([26 USC 6694](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6694)). Advice to a client about penalties must be "contemporaneously document[ed]". "A general disclaimer will not satisfy the requirement." The reasonable-cause defence weighs "normal office practice" ([26 CFR 1.6694-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.6694-2)). The closest thing US law has to a workpaper specification is **Treas. Reg. §1.6695-2**, the due-diligence rule behind **Form 8867**. It covers the earned income credit, the child tax credit, the American Opportunity credit and head-of-household status. Preparers must "contemporaneously document" each inquiry and response. They must keep the computation, "a record of how and when the information was obtained including the identity of who provided it", and copies of the documents relied on, for three years ([26 CFR 1.6695-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.6695-2)). The penalty is **$650 per failure** for returns filed in 2026 ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/node/146356)). One study guide gives $635; the IRS figure is used here. **§6107** requires keeping a copy or list of returns for three years ([26 USC 6107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6107)). **§7216** limits any use or disclosure of return information beyond preparing the return ([26 CFR 301.7216-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-2)).

**Retention** comes from these rules plus the statute of limitations (the deadline for the IRS to assess more tax). The IRS can generally assess for 3 years after filing. That becomes 6 years if more than 25% of gross income was left off, and there is no limit for fraud or for a return never filed ([26 USC 6501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6501)). Firms commonly keep files for **seven years**. That covers the 6-year window, and also the 7-year refund window for bad debts and worthless securities. Seven years is common practice, not a federal rule ([The Tax Adviser, Feb 2023](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/feb/documentation-and-recordkeeping-for-tax-practitioners)). State rules mostly cover client records, not tax workpapers. Texas requires original client records back within 10 business days. It requires work papers that hold "support used to reach final figures in tax returns" within 20, and it counts tax-software data files and their passwords as client records ([22 TAC §501.76](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/22-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-501-76)). California requires non-CPA preparers to keep return copies for four years ([Cal. BPC §22253](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_22253)). Whether that four-year rule binds CPAs is unverified.

| Record | Minimum retention | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Return copy or client list | 3 years after the return period (12 months from 1 July) | [26 CFR 1.6107-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.6107-1) |
| Form 8879 / 8878 | 3 years from due date or IRS receipt, whichever is later | [Pub 1345](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1345.pdf) |
| Form 8867 due-diligence file | 3 years from the latest of due date, e-file date, or presentation | [26 CFR 1.6695-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.6695-2) |
| Other customer data (FTC default) | Dispose of within 2 years of last use, unless law or business need requires keeping it | [16 CFR 314.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/314.4) |
| Workpapers (common practice) | About 7 years; basis and carryforward records for as long as the asset or carryforward lives | [The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/feb/documentation-and-recordkeeping-for-tax-practitioners) |

## Audit and IRS-examiner standards supply the vocabulary tax practice lacks

Tax has no documentation standard, so the best models come from audit and from the IRS's own examiners. The **experienced-auditor test** in AU-C 230 (non-public companies) and PCAOB AS 1215 (public companies) is the right acceptance test for the tool. Documentation must let an experienced practitioner with no prior connection to the work understand what was done, who did it and when, the evidence, the conclusions, and who reviewed it and when ([open-exam-prep summary of AU-C 230](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cpa-aud/final-evaluation-documentation/audit-documentation-retention)). Both standards also set a **completion date**, after which the file becomes append-only. Under AU-C 230 the final file must be assembled within **60 days**. After that nothing may be deleted, and any addition must record its reason, author, date and reviewer ([open-exam-prep](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cpa-aud/final-evaluation-documentation/audit-documentation-retention)). AS 1215 is moving from 45 days to 14 days alongside the PCAOB's new quality standard, QC 1000, which was postponed to 15 December 2026 ([Journal of Accountancy](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2025/aug/pcaob-postpones-effective-date-for-new-quality-control-system/)). The PCAOB site blocked every fetch, so AS 1215's paragraph wording and the effective date of the 14-day rule are unverified.

Two more audit standards transfer cleanly. **AU-C 500** ranks evidence by how reliable it is. Original documents beat copies, and information produced by the client needs evidence that it is accurate and complete ([HKICPA 500, conformed text](https://www.hkicpa.org.hk/-/media/HKICPA-Website/HKICPA/section6_standards/standards/sas500.pdf)). In 1040 terms, a third-party W-2, 1099 or K-1 outranks a client spreadsheet, which outranks a client's oral answer. **AU-C 520** (analytical procedures) gives the exact recipe for prior-year review. Form an expectation, such as last year's line adjusted for known changes. Set a threshold "before comparing it" to the recorded figure. Corroborate every breach, because "inquiry alone is NEVER sufficient" ([open-exam-prep AU-C 520](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cpa-aud/evidence-fundamentals/substantive-analytical-procedures)). Audit **materiality** (the size of error a reader would care about) and audit **sampling** carry over less well. In tax every dollar lands on the return, and software makes checking the whole population cheap. The tool should tie out every lot on a 1099-B by machine and keep human attention for judgement items.

The IRS examiner's own file follows the same pattern. It has an index (Form 4318). Every issue gets a lead sheet with "issue name, adjustments …, conclusion, facts, law" and the taxpayer's position. There is cross-referenced support with tick marks, and an **activity record** that "should be prepared contemporaneously" ([IRM 4.10.9](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-010-009)). Examiners weigh evidence by type. Documents have "great probative value". Oral testimony "need not be accepted without further inquiry". "'Per oral testimony' or 'as reasonable' are insufficient" unless the amounts are small ([IRM 4.10.7](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-010-007)). The most common notice, the **CP2000**, is sent when third-party income data "doesn't match what you reported" ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/understanding-your-cp2000-notice)). A tool that reconciles every W-2 and 1099 to its return line heads off that notice before filing.

For the lineage itself, **W3C PROV** (a 2013 W3C Recommendation) is the standard vocabulary for "where did this value come from". It defines provenance as information about "entities, activities, and people involved in producing a piece of data", used to judge "quality, reliability or trustworthiness" ([W3C PROV Overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)). In PROV terms, a source field such as 1099-B box 1d is an *Entity*. An extraction, computation or review is an *Activity*. A preparer, reviewer, OCR engine or client is an *Agent*. A return line is *wasDerivedFrom* its inputs. "Trace this number" then becomes a walk through a graph. The tool can borrow this shape without adopting RDF, the semantic-web format PROV is usually published in.

## E-file and security rules define what "freeze", "roles" and "log" must mean

**Pub 1345 (Rev. 12-2025)**, the IRS rulebook for e-file providers, is the strongest primary basis for "freeze at filing". After an electronic record is signed, "it must be tamper-proof … techniques must be employed that lock a document and prevent it from being changed". Signatures must be linked to their records so they cannot be removed or moved to another record ([Pub 1345](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1345.pdf)). It also gives the most concrete rule for reopening after a change. A new taxpayer signature is needed if, after signing, total income or AGI changes by more than **$50**, or total tax, withholding, refund or amount owed changes by more than **$14** ([Pub 1345](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1345.pdf)). The taxpayer signs Form 8879 "after reviewing the return and ensuring the tax return information on the form matches". E-signature software must record the image, timestamp, IP address, login, identity-check result and method. A return must not be e-filed before the ERO has the W-2, W-2G and 1099-R forms it relies on. A rejected return counts as not filed. The taxpayer must be told within 24 hours, and a timely return can be corrected and resubmitted within five calendar days of the due date ([Pub 1345](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1345.pdf)). So a reject should create a correction version that links back to the original. The frozen original is never edited.

**The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314)** names "an accountant or other tax preparation service" as a covered financial institution ([16 CFR 314.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/314.2)). It requires the following ([16 CFR 314.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/314.4)):

- access limited to what each user needs for their duties, with customers seeing only their own data;
- encryption in transit and at rest;
- **MFA** (multi-factor sign-in) "for any individual accessing any information system";
- controls to "monitor and log the activity of authorized users and detect … tampering";
- disposal of customer data within two years of last use;
- FTC notice within 30 days of a breach affecting 500 or more consumers.

Firms holding data on fewer than 5,000 consumers are excused from some paperwork, but not from MFA, encryption or logging ([16 CFR 314.6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/314.6)). Pub 1345 sets a shorter clock: security incidents must be reported to the IRS by the next business day ([Pub 1345](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1345.pdf)). A firm can only count affected clients that fast if the log records who *viewed* each SSN and bank number, not just who edited it. NIST SP 800-53 control **AU-9** adds that audit logs must be protected from "unauthorized access, modification, and deletion" ([UpGuard](https://www.upguard.com/compliance/nist-sp-800-53/au/au-9)). In practice, no role, not even admin, can delete a log entry.

**§7216 consent** sets the outer edge of the data model. Using or disclosing return information beyond preparing the return needs signed written consent. Use and disclosure need separate documents. A consent with no stated term lasts one year. A 1040 filer's SSN may not be disclosed to a preparer outside the US even with consent ([26 CFR 301.7216-3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-3)). Use inside the same firm and quality review by CPAs are exempt ([26 CFR 301.7216-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7216-2)), so the reviewer and partner roles need no consent. Commentators treat sending return data to a third-party AI vendor as a disclosure that needs consent ([The Tax Adviser, Jan 2024](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2024/jan/the-many-implications-of-sec-7216.html)), but the IRS has issued no AI-specific guidance. This matters only if the tool adds an AI feature.

No rule found requires WORM storage (write-once hardware) for tax preparers. The benchmark that does exist points to event sourcing. Since 2022 the SEC has let broker-dealers replace WORM with an audit trail that can recreate any original record and logs who created, changed or deleted what, and when ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/-/media/files/perspectives-events/publications/2022/10/legal-update--sec-adopts-amendments-to-electronic-recordkeeping-requirements-for-brokerdealers-and-sbs-entities.pdf)). An append-only event log meets that stricter standard, even though it does not bind CPA firms.

## Mapping each standard to a design requirement for the prototype

The tiering rule is simple. A requirement is a **must-have** for the one-day prototype if it is the visible substance of the core idea: provable numbers, an event log, review gates, reopening sign-offs, freeze. It is also a must-have if it is cheap and makes a packet mistake visible. It is **described-only** if it depends on real infrastructure, real identity or external systems (MFA, key management, IRS transmission, signature vendors), which a prototype on invented data would only fake.

| Standard or practice | What it demands | Design requirement | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead sheets, cross-references, IRM 4.10.9.8.2; W3C PROV | Every return line tied to its support | Every value is a claim with links back through computations to a source document, page and field; "trace this number" walks the graph | **Must-have** |
| Tick marks, IRM 4.10.9.8.2(3) | Consistent, legend-defined verification marks | Each value has a verification status from a fixed legend (tied to source, recomputed, agreed to prior year, client-confirmed, estimate, unverified), with who and when | **Must-have** |
| Experienced-reviewer test (AU-C 230 / AS 1215) | A stranger can see what, who, when, evidence, reviewer | Opening any number shows source, preparer, reviewer, timestamps, conclusion; anything not documented shows as "not done" | **Must-have** |
| SSTS §2.3, Circular 230 §10.34(d), Reg. §1.6695-2(b)(3) | Reasonable inquiry when data looks wrong; document question and answer at the time | Automated checks (on its face, across documents, against prior year); each fired check ends as a logged inquiry (question, answer, who answered, date, document relied on) or a documented dismissal | **Must-have** |
| AU-C 520; SSTS §2.3.9 | Prior-year comparison with a threshold set in advance; corroborated explanations | Prior-year column with $ and % change; firm-set threshold; each breach needs an explanation linked to evidence, not narrative alone | **Must-have** |
| AU-C 500; IRM 4.10.7 | Evidence ranked by reliability; oral answers alone are not enough | Each source carries a reliability rank (third-party document > client schedule > oral answer); a value backed only by an oral answer is flagged | **Must-have** |
| Review notes; Lacerte/CCH diagnostics; GitHub conversation resolution | Notes cleared before sign-off; fatal errors block e-file | Review notes are records (scope, owner, state, thread, resolution); open notes and fatal checks block filing; "acknowledged" is separate from "resolved" | **Must-have** |
| Sign-off levels (SPbinder); Circular 230 §10.22(b); GitHub stale-approval dismissal | Recorded preparer → reviewer → partner review | Sign-offs per item and per role; a change to any upstream value marks dependent sign-offs stale and reopens them | **Must-have** |

## Conclusion

The research turns a design taste into a compliance argument. Treating each 1040 number as "a claim that must be proven" is not an invention. It is the operational form of a duty that SSTS §2.3, Circular 230 §10.34(d) and Reg. §1.6695-2 all state in nearly the same words: rely in good faith, but inquire when something looks wrong, and write the inquiry down at the time. No US body prescribes the file's format. A tool that records sources, inquiries, reviews and locks by default therefore produces the evidence these duties call for, without imposing a template on the firm. The event log's strongest defence is that it matches the strictest benchmarks available. Those are the audit standards' rule that a completed file can only be added to, and Pub 1345's demand that a signed record be "tamper-proof". Neither strictly binds a tax-only firm, which shows how far the design exceeds what the law requires.

The traditional workflow's one structural defect is that sign-offs and notes are attached to pages, not numbers, so they go stale silently when late documents arrive. That is the gap the prototype should make visible in its demo. Show a corrected source value reopening exactly the sign-offs that depend on it, and an open review note stopping the filing. The security and e-file layers are real obligations. In a one-day build they belong in the write-up as a credible path, not as fake screens. The open questions worth flagging honestly are these: the exact AS 1215 wording and timing; whether California's four-year rule binds CPAs; and how §7216 applies to AI vendors. The prototype does not depend on any of them.
